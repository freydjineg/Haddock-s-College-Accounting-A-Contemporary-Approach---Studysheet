What is accounting?

Short read

Accounting is called the language of business because it’s how financial information gets communicated. Owners, managers, investors, and others use it to make decisions.

Definition to memorizeAccounting is the process by which financial information about a business is recorded, classified, summarized, interpreted, and communicated to owners, managers, and other interested parties.

The accounting system

An accounting system accumulates data about a firm’s financial affairs, classifies it in a meaningful way, and summarizes it in periodic reports called financial statements. The accountant:

  • establishes the records and procedures that make up the system,
  • supervises how the system operates, and
  • interprets the resulting financial information.

Hybrid forms

  • Limited liability partnership (LLP): partners are liable for their own actions and those they supervise, but not another partner’s.
  • S corporation: a corporation taxed essentially like a partnership. Owners get limited liability and avoid double taxation.
  • Limited liability company (LLC): limited liability for all owners, unlimited number of owners, and it can elect to be taxed as a partnership (Form 1065), a corporation (Form 1120), or an S corporation (Form 1120S).

A creditor is a person, company, or government agency the business owes money to. Privately owned (closely held) corporations don’t trade stock on an exchange; publicly owned ones do.

Generally accepted accounting principles

Short read

The SEC delegated standard-setting to the profession, which developed GAAP. Publicly owned companies must follow GAAP unless doing so would be misleading.

How the FASB issued standards (before 2009)

  1. Discussion memorandum explains the topic
  2. Public hearings: the SEC, AICPA, public accounting firms, AAA (educators), and interested businesses comment
  3. Exposure draft describes the proposed statement; public comments are evaluated
  4. Vote: at least 4 of 5 members must approve to issue a Statement of Financial Accounting Standards (168 were issued)
Since July 1, 2009The FASB Accounting Standards Codification is the single source of authoritative U.S. GAAP, organized into about 90 topics. Changes come through an Accounting Standards Update.

Each year publicly traded companies submit financial statements audited by independent CPAs. The auditor’s report gives an opinion on fair presentation and confirms GAAP conformity. International accounting studies other countries’ principles; the IASB (formerly IASC, formed 1973) works toward comparable standards.

Managerial implications
  • Managers make sure the accounting system produces timely, accurate, fair information.
  • Statements for outsiders follow GAAP; public companies file audited statements with the SEC every year.
  • Internal reports for managers don’t have to follow GAAP, but they must be useful for monitoring and controlling operations.

Rules to remember

Quick review before the practice questions
  • Accounting = record, classify, summarize, interpret, communicate.
  • Three practice areas: public, managerial (private), governmental.
  • Public accounting services: auditing, tax accounting, management advisory services.
  • The SEC has the final say on financial reporting for public corporations; the FASB writes GAAP.
  • Sole proprietors and partners are personally liable; stockholders risk only their investment.
  • Every business is a separate entity for accounting; only corporations are separate legal entities.
  • FASB steps: discussion memorandum, hearings, exposure draft, vote (4 of 5).

Practice questions

Try each one before you reveal the answer
1.What is the purpose of accounting?
Show answer

Answer: To gather and communicate financial information about a business so people can make decisions.

Why: Picture running a shop with no records. You wouldn’t know how much cash you have, who owes you, or whether you made money. Accounting collects every money event, organizes it, and turns it into reports that owners, managers, banks, and others can read. Without it, every decision would be a guess.

Key takeaway: Accounting turns raw money events into information for decisions.
2.What is the purpose of the auditor’s report?
Show answer

Answer: To give an outside professional’s objective opinion that the statements fairly present the results and financial position, and were prepared according to GAAP.

Why: A company prepares its own statements, so readers might wonder if they’re honest. An independent CPA, someone who doesn’t work for the company and has no money in it, examines the records and states an opinion. It’s like a home inspector’s report: a neutral expert telling you whether what you’re looking at is sound.

Key takeaway: The auditor’s report adds outside credibility to the company’s numbers.
3.How is ownership of a corporation different from a sole proprietorship?
Show answer

Answer: A sole proprietorship has one owner who is personally responsible for its debts. A corporation is a separate legal entity owned by stockholders, who can lose only what they invested.

Why: If a sole proprietorship can’t pay its bills, creditors can go after the owner’s personal savings, car, or house. A corporation is legally its own “person,” so its debts belong to it, not to the stockholders. Stockholders risk only the money they paid for their shares. Ownership is also measured differently: a corporation’s owners hold shares, and their percentage depends on how many shares they own.

Key takeaway: Sole proprietor = unlimited personal risk; stockholder = risk limited to investment.
4.Why keep business records separate from the owner’s personal records, and what is this called?
Show answer

Answer: Mixing them makes it impossible to measure how the business is really doing. Keeping them apart is the separate entity assumption.

Why: Suppose the owner pays a personal grocery bill from the business account. The business would look like it spent more than it did, and profit would be understated. By treating the business as its own separate unit, the records show only business activity. This applies to all three business forms for accounting purposes, even though only a corporation is legally separate.

Key takeaway: The business’s books track only the business.
5.An employee makes an honest error recording a transaction. Is that fraud?
Show answer

Answer: No.

Why: Fraud requires an intentional or reckless act to take assets or misstate the records. An honest mistake, such as typing $357 instead of $375, has no intent behind it. It’s simply an error, and it’s fixed with a correcting entry (you’ll learn these in Chapter 4). Internal controls help catch both errors and fraud, but the two are treated very differently.

Key takeaway: Intent is the difference between fraud and error.
6.Name two common internal control procedures.
Show answer

Answer: (1) Written proof that transactions and payments are authorized. (2) Separating duties among employees.

Why: Requiring written approval means no money leaves without someone signing off, which creates a paper trail. Separating duties means one person can’t do every step of a job alone. For example, the person who collects cash shouldn’t also record it, because then they could pocket some and hide it in the books. Splitting the work means stealing would require two people working together, which is much less likely.

Key takeaway: Authorize everything, and never let one person control a whole process.

2-1

Property and financial interest

Long read

A business transaction is any financial event that changes the resources of a firm: purchases, sales, payments, and receipts of cash. Accounting starts by analyzing each one.

Property = Financial Interest

In a free enterprise system all property is owned by someone, so the two sides always balance. The owner’s financial interest is called equity or capital.

How to analyze a transaction

  1. Describe the financial event: identify the property, who owns it, and the amount of increase or decrease.
  2. Make sure the equation is still in balance.

Order of preparation

  1. Income statement: net income moves to the statement of owner’s equity
  2. Statement of owner’s equity: ending capital moves to the balance sheet
  3. Balance sheet: a snapshot on a single date (Eli: total assets $136,850 = liabilities $3,500 + capital $133,350)
Snapshot versus movieThe balance sheet is a snapshot on one date. The income statement is like a movie covering a period of time.
Managerial implications
  • Sound records and statements are necessary for good decisions.
  • Statements show the profit or loss, assets on hand, amounts owed to creditors, and owner’s equity.
  • Well-run businesses have accounting systems that give timely, useful information.
  • Revenue and expense transactions are recorded separately from owner’s equity so each period’s operations can be analyzed.

Rules to remember

Quick review before the practice questions
  • Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity, after every transaction.
  • Revenue increases owner’s equity; expenses and withdrawals decrease it.
  • Collecting accounts receivable does not create revenue.
  • Paying a creditor decreases both assets and liabilities.
  • Statement order: income statement, statement of owner’s equity, balance sheet.
  • Ending capital = beginning capital + additional investments + net income − withdrawals.

Practice questions

Try each one before you reveal the answer
1.What’s the difference between buying for cash and buying on account?
Show answer

Answer: Buying for cash reduces Cash right away. Buying on account creates a liability, Accounts Payable, that you pay later.

Why: In both cases you get the item, so an asset goes up. The difference is what happens on the other side. With cash, another asset (Cash) goes down, so total assets don’t change. On account, you haven’t paid yet, so you now owe the seller, and liabilities go up. Later, when you pay, Cash and Accounts Payable both go down.

Key takeaway: Cash purchase = swap assets. Purchase on account = asset up, liability up.
2.If one side of the accounting equation increases, what happens to the other side?
Show answer

Answer: It must increase by the same amount.

Why: Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity is always true because every asset was supplied by someone: creditors or the owner. If assets go up by $5,000, someone must have a $5,000 bigger claim. For example, buying equipment on credit raises assets and liabilities equally; earning revenue raises assets and owner’s equity equally. (Some transactions change only one side, like trading cash for supplies, but then the changes on that side cancel out.)

Key takeaway: The equation always balances after every transaction.
3.Describe a transaction that decreases Accounts Receivable and increases Cash by $2,800.
Show answer

Answer: A customer pays $2,800 on their account.

Why: Accounts Receivable is money customers owe you from earlier sales on credit. When one of them pays, you get cash (Cash goes up) and they owe you less (Accounts Receivable goes down). Total assets stay the same, and no revenue is recorded, because the revenue was already counted when the original sale happened.

Key takeaway: Collecting from customers swaps one asset for another; it isn’t new revenue.
4.Why is the income statement prepared before the statement of owner’s equity?
Show answer

Answer: The statement of owner’s equity needs the net income (or net loss) figure, which comes from the income statement.

Why: The statement of owner’s equity works like this: beginning capital + net income − withdrawals = ending capital. You can’t fill in the middle line until you’ve calculated net income. Then the ending capital flows to the balance sheet. That’s why the order is always income statement → statement of owner’s equity → balance sheet.

Key takeaway: Each statement hands a number to the next one.
5.Home Interiors has assets of $120,000 and liabilities of $95,000. What is owner’s equity?
Show answer

Answer: $25,000.

How: Start with Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity. Rearrange to Owner’s Equity = Assets − Liabilities. Then $120,000 − $95,000 = $25,000.

Why it makes sense: If the business sold all its assets for $120,000 and paid off the $95,000 it owes, $25,000 would be left for the owner. That leftover is the owner’s equity.

Key takeaway: Owner’s equity is what’s left after all debts are paid.
6.Why does the third heading line differ between the balance sheet and the income statement?
Show answer

Answer: The balance sheet is for a single date; the income statement covers a period of time.

Why: The balance sheet is like a photograph: it shows what the business owns and owes at one moment, so its heading says “December 31, 20X1.” The income statement is like a video: it shows revenue and expenses that piled up over time, so its heading says “Month Ended December 31, 20X1.” Mixing these up is a common test mistake.

Key takeaway: Balance sheet = “on [date]”; income statement = “for the period ended [date].”

Accounts and T accounts

Short read

The accounting equation table from Chapter 2 is too clumsy for a business with thousands of transactions a month. Instead, businesses keep a separate record for each item, called an account. Accounts are recognized by their classification:

  • Asset accounts show the property a business owns.
  • Liability accounts show its debts.
  • Owner’s equity accounts show the owner’s financial interest.

Account balances and normal balances

Short read

An account balance is the difference between the two sides of an account:

  1. Add each side. If a side has more than one figure, write the total in small pencil figures called a footing.
  2. Subtract the smaller total from the larger.
  3. Write the balance on the side with the larger total.

If an account has only one amount, that amount is the balance. If it has entries on only one side, the total of those entries is the balance.

Practice questions

Try each one before you reveal the answer
1.What is the normal balance of Accounts Payable, and why?
Show answer

Answer: Credit.

Why: Accounts Payable is a liability, money the business owes. Liabilities are on the right side of the accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity). The rule is that an account increases on the same side it sits in the equation, so liabilities increase on the right, the credit side. Because the account usually grows more than it shrinks, its balance normally sits on the credit side too.

Key takeaway: Right side of the equation = increase with a credit = normal credit balance.
2.The owner withdraws $1,000. Which accounts are debited and credited?
Show answer

Answer: Debit Owner’s Drawing $1,000; credit Cash $1,000.

Why: Cash, an asset, goes down, and assets decrease with credits. The withdrawal also reduces the owner’s claim on the business. Instead of debiting Capital directly, businesses use a separate Drawing account so withdrawals are easy to see. Drawing increases with a debit because it represents a decrease in owner’s equity.

Key takeaway: Withdrawals: debit Drawing, credit Cash. Never an expense.
3.Your friend’s trial balance shows debits of $91,000 and credits of $104,000. What steps would you take to find the error?
Show answer

Answer: (1) Re-add both columns. (2) Check that every balance is in the correct column. (3) Recompute each account balance. (4) Trace each transaction back to how it was recorded.

Why: Start with the easiest errors: simple addition mistakes. Then look for a balance put in the wrong column, a math slip inside an account, or a transaction recorded incorrectly. The difference here is $13,000. Divided by 2 that’s $6,500, so look for a $6,500 amount on the wrong side. $13,000 isn’t divisible by 9, so a transposition or slide is less likely.

Key takeaway: Work from simplest error to hardest, and use the ÷2 and ÷9 clues.
4.Why are revenue accounts increased by credits?
Show answer

Answer: Revenue increases owner’s equity, and owner’s equity increases on the credit side.

Why: Revenue is money earned by the business, and earning money makes the owner’s share bigger. Since revenue is really a part of owner’s equity (kept in its own account so we can measure it), it follows owner’s equity’s rule: credit to increase. Expenses do the reverse: they shrink owner’s equity, so they increase with debits.

Key takeaway: Revenue acts like owner’s equity: credit to increase.
5.Is Fees Income a permanent or temporary account?
Show answer

Answer: Temporary.

Why: Fees Income measures revenue for one period, such as a month or year. At the end of the period its balance is moved into the owner’s capital account (closing, Chapter 6), and it starts the new period at zero. That way each period’s revenue is measured separately. Balance sheet accounts like Cash are permanent; their balances carry over.

Key takeaway: Revenue, expense, and drawing accounts are temporary.
6.Cash has debits of $8,600 and credits of $3,250. What is its balance?
Show answer

Answer: $5,350 debit.

How: Subtract the smaller side from the larger side: $8,600 − $3,250 = $5,350. The debit side is larger, so the balance is a debit.

Does it make sense? Cash is an asset, and assets normally have debit balances. A debit balance means there’s still $5,350 in the bank. A credit balance in Cash would mean you spent more than you had, which signals an error.

Key takeaway: Balance = bigger side − smaller side, placed on the bigger side.

Posting to the general ledger

Long read

The ledger is the record of final entry. The general ledger is the permanent, classified record of every account. Posting transfers journal data to the ledger and is step 3 of the accounting cycle.

A balance ledger form shows the account’s balance after every posting, with columns for Date, Description, Post. Ref., Debit, Credit, and Debit/Credit Balance.

Five posting steps

  1. On the ledger form, enter the date (and a description if needed).
  2. Enter the journal page in Post. Ref. (J1 = general journal page 1).
  3. Enter the amount in the Debit or Credit column.
  4. Compute the new balance and enter it in the Debit or Credit Balance column.
  5. Back in the journal, enter the ledger account number in Post. Ref.
Why both posting references?They show the entry was posted, prevent posting it twice, and let you trace an amount from ledger to journal to source document.

Ledger order matches the trial balance: assets, liabilities, owner’s equity, revenue, expenses. That speeds up preparing the trial balance and statements.

Practice questions

Try each one before you reveal the answer
1.Supplies has a $2,400 balance and $900 is on hand. What adjustment is made?
Show answer

Answer: Debit Supplies Expense $1,500; credit Supplies $1,500.

How: Supplies used = what you had − what’s left = $2,400 − $900 = $1,500.

Why: Supplies sit on the books as an asset until they’re used. The $1,500 that was used is now a cost of doing business, so it moves to Supplies Expense. After the adjustment, Supplies shows $900, which matches what’s actually on the shelf.

Key takeaway: The adjustment is for the amount used. The amount on hand is the new balance.
2.A machine costs $36,000, has a $6,000 salvage value, and a 5-year life. Annual straight-line depreciation?
Show answer

Answer: $6,000 per year.

How: Step 1: Cost − Salvage = $36,000 − $6,000 = $30,000. This is the part of the cost the business will use up. Step 2: $30,000 ÷ 5 years = $6,000 a year.

Why subtract salvage? You expect to sell the machine for $6,000 at the end, so that part isn’t really “used up.” Only $30,000 is a true cost spread over the machine’s life.

Key takeaway: (Cost − Salvage) ÷ Life.
3.Why isn’t depreciation credited directly to the Equipment account?
Show answer

Answer: So the original cost stays visible. Accumulated Depreciation, a contra asset, holds the total depreciation separately.

Why: Readers want to know both what the equipment cost and how much has been used up. If depreciation reduced Equipment directly, the original cost would disappear. Keeping them separate shows Equipment $11,000, less Accumulated Depreciation $183, equals book value $10,817.

Key takeaway: Equipment keeps its cost; the contra account tracks depreciation.
4.On December 1 a firm paid $98,400 for six months of insurance. What’s the December 31 adjustment?
Show answer

Answer: Debit Insurance Expense $16,400; credit Prepaid Insurance $16,400.

How: Cost per month = $98,400 ÷ 6 = $16,400. One month (December) has passed, so one month’s worth has expired.

Why: On December 1 the whole $98,400 was recorded as an asset, because it bought future protection. By December 31, one month of that protection is used up and becomes an expense. The other $82,000 stays as Prepaid Insurance for the next five months.

Key takeaway: Expired amount = cost per month × months passed.
5.In which worksheet columns does net income appear?
Show answer

Answer: The Income Statement Debit column and the Balance Sheet Credit column.

Why: With a profit, the Income Statement credits (revenue) total more than the debits (expenses). Adding net income to the debit side makes that pair balance. Net income also increases owner’s equity, and equity increases with a credit, so it goes in the Balance Sheet Credit column, which makes that pair balance too.

Key takeaway: Net income balances both pairs: IS Debit, BS Credit. (A net loss goes the opposite way.)
6.Net income before adjustments is $270,000. Adjustments: expired rent $21,000, depreciation $24,600, supplies used $10,800. Correct net income?
Show answer

Answer: $213,600.

How: Total adjustments = $21,000 + $24,600 + $10,800 = $56,400. Each is an expense that wasn’t recorded yet. $270,000 − $56,400 = $213,600.

Why it matters: Skipping adjustments would overstate profit by $56,400. The owner would think the business earned much more than it did, and the asset accounts (Prepaid Rent, Equipment, Supplies) would also be overstated.

Key takeaway: These adjustments add expenses, so they lower net income.

The closing process and Income Summary

Short read

  1. Transfer the results of operations (net income or net loss) to owner’s equity.

The four steps

  2. Transfer the expense account balances to Income Summary.

Practice questions

Try each one before you reveal the answer
1.A firm has Rent Expense $3,600, Salaries Expense $7,000, and Supplies Expense $1,500. Give the entry to close the expenses.
Show answer

Answer: Debit Income Summary $12,100; credit Rent Expense $3,600, Salaries Expense $7,000, and Supplies Expense $1,500.

How: Each expense has a debit balance. To bring it to zero, credit it for exactly its balance. Add the three: $3,600 + $7,000 + $1,500 = $12,100, and debit that total to Income Summary. It’s one compound entry: one debit, three credits.

Key takeaway: Close expenses by crediting each one; the total goes to Income Summary as a debit.
2.Income Summary has a $4,200 debit balance after closing revenue and expenses. What does that mean, and what’s the entry?
Show answer

Answer: A net loss of $4,200. Debit Owner’s Capital $4,200; credit Income Summary $4,200.

Why: Revenue was closed to the credit side and expenses to the debit side. A debit balance means expenses were bigger, so the business lost money. A loss reduces the owner’s equity, and equity decreases with a debit, so debit Capital. Crediting Income Summary brings it to zero.

Key takeaway: Debit balance in Income Summary = net loss = debit Capital.
3.Which accounts appear on a postclosing trial balance?
Show answer

Answer: Only permanent accounts: assets, contra assets such as Accumulated Depreciation, liabilities, and owner’s capital.

Why: Closing brought every temporary account (revenue, expenses, drawing, and Income Summary) to zero. A zero balance has nothing to list. The textbook’s true/false item is a common trap: “all owner’s equity accounts appear” is false, because Drawing and Income Summary are owner’s equity accounts but are temporary.

Key takeaway: Postclosing trial balance = balance sheet accounts only.
4.Where does the information for closing entries come from?
Show answer

Answer: The worksheet, mainly the Income Statement columns (revenue and expenses) and the Balance Sheet columns (drawing).

Why: The worksheet already lists every revenue and expense with its adjusted balance, plus net income. Using it means you don’t have to hunt through the ledger. The amounts on the worksheet should match the ledger after adjustments are posted.

Key takeaway: The worksheet is your road map for closing.
5.Why isn’t Drawing closed to Income Summary?
Show answer

Answer: Withdrawals aren’t expenses, so they must not affect net income.

Why: Income Summary’s balance is net income or loss. If Drawing went through it, the owner taking money for personal use would look like the business performed worse, which it didn’t. Withdrawals reduce the owner’s claim directly, so Drawing closes straight to Capital: debit Capital, credit Drawing.

Key takeaway: Drawing bypasses Income Summary and goes straight to Capital.
6.List the last three steps of the accounting cycle.
Show answer

Answer: (7) Journalize and post closing entries; (8) prepare a postclosing trial balance; (9) interpret the financial information.

Why this order: Closing resets the temporary accounts. The postclosing trial balance then proves the ledger still balances after all those entries. Finally, the information is interpreted and used to make decisions, which is the whole reason for keeping records.

Key takeaway: Close → prove → interpret.

The accounts receivable ledger

Short read

Accounts receivable is a big asset for many businesses, and it must turn into cash on time, or a profitable firm can still run short of money to pay its bills. Management uses customer detail to check that customers pay on time and stay within credit limits, and to answer customer questions about balances or billing errors.

A subsidiary ledger holds accounts of one type with the detail behind a general ledger account. The accounts receivable ledger has one account per credit customer, usually in alphabetical order. Post to it daily so each customer’s balance is always known.

Accounts Receivable in the general ledger is a control account: its balance must equal the total of the subsidiary ledger.

Customer accounts use a three-column balance form (Debit, Credit, Balance) with no Dr/Cr balance column, because customer balances are assumed to be debits. If a customer overpays, the credit balance is shown in parentheses or circled. Small firms alphabetize customer accounts; larger ones number them.

In the journal, add the customer’s name after the account: “Accounts Receivable/Ann Anh.” When posting to a customer account, put a check mark (✓) in the journal’s Post. Ref. column next to the account number, for example “111/✓”.

Schedule of accounts receivableAt month-end, after all posting, list every customer with a balance and total them. Compare the total with the Accounts Receivable control account; if they differ, find and fix the errors.

  2. → individual balances listed on the schedule
  3. → schedule total must equal Accounts Receivable in the general ledger

5.A $500 sale plus 6% tax is paid with a bank credit card; the bank charges a 3% fee. What’s the debit to Cash?
Show answer

Answer: $514.10.

How: Step 1: tax = $500 × 6% = $30, so the card is charged $530. Step 2: fee = $530 × 3% = $15.90 (the fee is on the total, tax included). Step 3: Cash = $530 − $15.90 = $514.10.

Full entry: Debit Cash $514.10 and Credit Card Expense $15.90; credit Sales $500 and Sales Tax Payable $30.

Key takeaway: Bank card = cash sale; the fee is an expense on sale + tax.
6.What is a control account, and why is it useful to have an accounts receivable ledger?
Show answer

Answer: A control account links a subsidiary ledger to the general ledger; its balance equals the total of the subsidiary accounts. The accounts receivable ledger shows exactly what each customer owes at all times.

Why both? The general ledger needs one tidy total for the trial balance and balance sheet. But for billing, answering customer questions, and deciding whether to give more credit, you need each customer’s details. The control account gives the total; the subsidiary ledger gives the detail. The schedule of accounts receivable proves they agree.

Key takeaway: Control account = total; subsidiary ledger = details by customer.

The purchasing process and internal control

Long read

Most merchandisers buy on credit under open-account arrangements. A large firm usually has a centralized purchasing department that finds suppliers, gets price quotes, negotiates credit terms, and places orders. In a small firm the owner or manager usually handles purchasing. Home Depot’s purchasing department buys tens of thousands of different products.

Documents in the purchasing cycle

  1. Purchase requisition: the sales department lists items it needs and sends it to purchasing. It’s signed by someone authorized to approve requests, usually the sales manager. (Maxx-Out’s men’s department requested 10 cartons of T-shirts and shorts.)
  2. Purchase order: purchasing picks a supplier with a competitive price and orders the exact items, quantity, price, and credit terms. It’s signed by an authorized person, usually the purchasing agent.
  3. Receiving report: goods are inspected on arrival and the quantity and condition are recorded. Purchasing compares it to the purchase order and settles any wrong or defective shipments with the supplier.
  4. Purchase invoice: the supplier’s bill. To the supplier it’s a sales invoice.
  5. Accounting compares the invoice with the purchase order and receiving report, checks quantities, prices, and math, and records the purchase as soon as it’s verified. Shortly before the due date, it issues a check and records the payment.
Internal control over purchases
  • Purchases are authorized and processed with proper documents.
  • Separate duties: different people request, order, receive, record, and pay.
  • Pay only from an approved invoice that matches the purchase order and receiving report.
  • Choose suppliers carefully and check goods when they arrive.
Employee fraudThe Association of Certified Fraud Examiners reports that a typical organization loses a meaningful share of its revenue to fraud each year. Good internal controls are the best defense.

Some firms use a voucher system for extra control. With or without one, controls should create written proof that purchases and payments are authorized, and make sure different people buy, receive, and pay. The COSO framework notes that small companies may implement controls differently; there, management’s own commitment sets the tone (the control environment).

Seven controls of an effective system

  1. Make purchases only after written authorization.
  2. Check goods carefully on arrival; compare them to the purchase order and invoice.
  3. Confirm the purchase order, receiving report, and invoice agree.
  4. Check the invoice computations.
  5. Have someone other than the person who ordered authorize payment, only after all checks.
  6. Have another person write the check.
  7. Use prenumbered requisitions, purchase orders, and checks, and account for every number.
Fraud defensesHave several employees in contact with suppliers, and screen both employees and vendors.
Why separation mattersIf one person could order, receive, and pay, they could buy items for themselves or pay fake invoices without anyone noticing.

The accounts payable ledger

Short read

Buying on credit lets a business operate on a bigger scale and use its money more effectively, but invoices must be paid on time to keep a good credit reputation. Posting to the general ledger works as in Chapter 4. The accounts payable ledger is a subsidiary ledger with a separate account for each creditor, posted daily. These accounts have credit balances, so the three-column form doesn’t label the balance. A debit balance (overpayment, or goods returned after paying) is circled or put in parentheses.

In the journal, write “Accounts Payable/Modern Sportsman” and mark the posting with “/✓” after the account number. Small firms alphabetize creditor accounts; larger and computerized systems number them and produce accounts payable aging reports that show how old each invoice is, in 30-day groups.

Schedule of accounts payableA month-end list of all balances owed to creditors. Its total must equal the Accounts Payable control account in the general ledger.
Managerial implicationsManagement and accounting work together on purchasing controls. Checks and balances protect against fraud, errors, and buying too much inventory. The A/P ledger keeps creditor information current, and prompt payment protects the firm’s credit reputation.

Practice questions

Try each one before you reveal the answer
1.Name the four documents in the purchasing process, in order, and what each does.
Show answer

Answer: (1) Purchase requisition, (2) purchase order, (3) receiving report, (4) purchase invoice.

What each does: The requisition is an internal request from a department. The purchase order is the official order sent to the supplier with items, quantities, prices, and terms. The receiving report records what actually arrived and its condition. The invoice is the supplier’s bill.

Why it matters: Before paying, compare the invoice to the receiving report (were we billed only for what arrived?) and to the purchase order (were we charged the agreed price?).

Key takeaway: Request → order → receive → bill, then match before paying.
2.An invoice is $5,000 of goods plus $150 freight, terms 2/10, n/30, paid within 10 days. How much is paid?
Show answer

Answer: $5,050.

How: Step 1: the discount applies only to the goods: $5,000 × 2% = $100. Step 2: total invoice = $5,000 + $150 = $5,150. Step 3: payment = $5,150 − $100 = $5,050.

Entry: Debit Accounts Payable $5,150; credit Purchases Discounts $100 and Cash $5,050.

Key takeaway: Never take the discount on freight.
3.Purchases $40,000, Freight In $1,200, Returns $600, Discounts $700. What’s the net delivered cost?
Show answer

Answer: $39,900.

How: Start with what you bought ($40,000), add the cost to ship it in (+$1,200 = $41,200), then subtract what you sent back (−$600 = $40,600) and what you saved by paying early (−$700 = $39,900).

Why it matters: This is the true cost of the merchandise bought this period, and it feeds into cost of goods sold on the income statement.

Key takeaway: Purchases + freight − returns − discounts.
4.What type of account is Purchases Returns and Allowances, and what is its normal balance?
Show answer

Answer: A contra expense (contra cost of goods sold) account with a credit balance.

Why: Purchases is debited when goods are bought. Returns reduce those purchases, so they’re recorded on the opposite side, the credit side, in their own account. Keeping returns separate lets managers see how often goods are sent back, which might signal a bad supplier.

Key takeaway: Contra accounts carry the opposite balance of the account they reduce.
5.What is the difference between a receiving report and an invoice?
Show answer

Answer: The receiving report shows the quantity and condition of goods that arrived. The invoice shows quantities and prices being billed.

Why both: The accounts payable clerk compares the invoice’s quantities to the receiving report, so the business pays only for goods actually received. Then the clerk compares the invoice’s prices to the purchase order, so the business isn’t overcharged. Payment is made from the verified invoice.

Key takeaway: Receiving report proves delivery; invoice states the charge.
6.Under a perpetual system, what two entries are made when merchandise costing $300 is sold on account for $500?
Show answer

Answer: (1) Debit Accounts Receivable $500; credit Sales $500. (2) Debit Cost of Goods Sold $300; credit Merchandise Inventory $300.

Why two entries: The first records the revenue: what the customer owes. The second records the cost: the item left the shelf, so inventory goes down and its cost becomes an expense. That second entry is what keeps a perpetual system’s inventory current at all times. (Sales tax, if any, would be added to the first entry.)

Key takeaway: Perpetual sale = record the price and record the cost.

The petty cash fund

Short read

Small payments (postage, a few office supplies) come from a petty cash fund kept by one person.

Most bills should be paid by check, but some small purchases can’t wait for approval and a check, like a $4 folder needed in two hours. Usually the office manager, cashier, or an assistant controls the fund.

Internal control of cash

Short read

Cash is the asset most easily stolen, lost, or mishandled.

Accountants design, set up, and monitor cash controls tailored to the business. A good system has one employee approve payments, another prepare checks, and another record them.

Controlling cash receipts

  • Only designated, carefully chosen and trained employees receive and handle cash (often bonded).
  • Separate duties: the person who handles cash shouldn’t also record it.
  • Deposit all receipts daily and intact.
  • Make all payments by check or electronic transfer, except from a carefully controlled petty cash fund.
  • Keep receipts in a register, locked drawer, or safe; record every receipt as it arrives (register audit tape locked inside until a supervisor removes it, or numbered sales slips).
  • Before depositing, have someone other than the receiver or recorder check the funds against the record.
  • Deposit promptly and intact; the depositor isn’t the person who receives or records the funds.
  • Record receipts promptly; the recorder doesn’t receive or deposit them.
  • Have the bank statement sent to and reconciled by someone who doesn’t handle, record, or deposit cash.
Real fraud case: Kavita HarackThe Department of Justice reported that an accounting employee in Florida directed dozens of fraudulent payments from her employer into bank accounts in her or her husband’s name, disguising them as vendor payments. After being fired, she did the same at a second company. She spent the money on a home, travel, and shopping, and was sentenced to federal prison for wire fraud and ordered to repay the victims. Separating who approves, records, and makes payments is exactly what stops this.

Controlling cash payments

  • Pay by check except from special funds like petty cash.
  • Issue checks only with an approved bill or invoice, approved by experienced, designated people.
  • One person prepares and records checks, another approves, another signs and mails. Consider two signatures above a set amount.
  • Use prenumbered checks and account for every number.
  • The person reconciling the bank statement doesn’t write or record checks; compare canceled checks to the check register.
  • Record payments promptly.
  • Reconcile the bank statement every month.
  • Bonding: an insurance company investigates employees who handle cash and insures against theft by them.

Quick review before the practice questions

Show answer

Wage and Hour Law (Fair Labor Standards Act)

  • Applies to employers in interstate commerce.
  • Sets a federal minimum wage ($7.25 when the book was printed) and 40 hours a week at the regular rate. Some states set higher minimums; Washington led in 2021 at $13.69, and employers must pay the higher rate.

  • Hours over 40 must be paid at time and a half (1.5 × regular rate).
  • Exempt employees (salaried supervisors and managers) aren’t covered by the overtime rules.

Social security and Medicare

The Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) is the Social Security Act. Its Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance (OASDI) program pays retirement benefits, benefits for a retired worker’s dependents, and disability benefits for the worker and dependents. Medicare was part of social security until 1992. The employer withholds both from employees and pays the withheld amount plus an equal match.

State and local income taxes: many states and some cities have them. Withholding works almost like federal, but each needs its own liability account because it’s paid to a different government.

10-5

Federal income tax withholding

Short read

Federal income tax is a pay-as-you-go tax. Employers withhold an estimate each payday and deposit it regularly. After year-end the employee files a return and either pays the balance or gets a refund. Self-employed people pay through estimated taxes, often using EFTPS.

Income tax withheld depends on the employee’s Form W-4 (Employee’s Withholding Certificate), which gives filing status (single or married filing separately; married filing jointly or qualifying surviving spouse; head of household), the number of jobs held, and whether a spouse works. The W-4 was overhauled for 2021; new employees use the new form. The employer uses it plus IRS guidance (Publication 15, Circular E, and the withholding tables). The employer withholds the tax and pays it to the government periodically.

Other required withholdings can include state and local income taxes, disability taxes in some states, and garnishments: court-ordered amounts for child support or other debts that the employer must withhold and send on.

Deductions not required by law

Made by agreement between employee and employer: group life or medical insurance, retirement plans, credit union savings or loan payments, savings bonds, stock purchase plans, employer loan repayments, and union dues. Each is recorded as a liability on payday and debited when paid. SB Express pays employees’ medical premiums but deducts $40 a week for spouse or child coverage (Dunlap and Wu).

Completing the payroll register (columns A–T)

  1. A, B, E: name, marital status, pay rate (entered once in software).
  2. C: cumulative earnings before this period (needed for the social security and FUTA limits).
  3. D: hours worked, from the time sheet.
  4. F, G, H: regular pay, overtime pay, gross pay.
  5. I: cumulative earnings after this period (C + H).
  6. J, K, L: taxable wages for social security, Medicare, and FUTA (only amounts under each limit).
  7. M, N, O, P: deductions for social security, Medicare, income tax, and health insurance.
  8. Q: net amount (H − deductions).
  9. R: check number.
  10. S, T: distribution to Office Salaries or Shipping Wages.

Then total the columns and cross-check: regular + overtime = gross; gross − deductions = net; office + shipping = gross.

Internal control over payroll

Payroll is usually a business’s largest expense, so controls must be strong:

  1. Use only responsible, well-trained payroll staff.
  2. Keep payroll records secure and confidential.

Reversing entries

Short read

Reversing entries, made on the first day of the new period, undo certain adjustments so routine transactions can be recorded normally. They’re optional, and are usually used only for accrued expenses and accrued income that will involve a future cash payment or receipt.

Without a reversing entryThe January 3 entry would have to be: debit Salaries Expense—Sales $500, debit Salaries Payable $1,200, credit Cash $1,700. The accountant must look back at the adjustments to split it, which takes time, causes errors, and is sometimes forgotten.
Unusual but correctRight after the January 1 reversal, Salaries Payable is zero and Salaries Expense has a credit balance. That’s fine; the January 3 payroll debit brings it to the right amount.
Never reversedUncollectible accounts, depreciation, inventory, and prepaid expenses recorded first as assets. None involve a later cash payment that would cause double counting. Exception: if prepaid expenses were recorded first as expenses (the alternative method), the year-end adjustment is reversed.

The accounting cycle for a merchandiser

The same nine steps as Chapter 6 (plus optional reversing entries, which simplify recording cash payments for accrued expenses and receipts for accrued income), now with more detail: source documents (sales slips, purchase invoices, credit memos, check stubs) feed the journals; posting goes to the general ledger and subsidiary ledgers; the worksheet adds inventory, accrual, and deferral adjustments; then statements, adjusting entries, closing entries, the postclosing trial balance, interpretation, and optional reversing entries.

How the accounting system fits together

A system with special journals and subsidiary ledgers has specialized areas:

  • Accounts receivable: sales and cash receipts, customer accounts, and billing.
  • Accounts payable: purchases and cash payments, and creditor accounts.
  • General ledger and financial reporting: the general journal, general ledger, end-of-period work, and statements. It’s the focal point, because every transaction eventually flows into the general ledger, which feeds the statements.
About accounting: professional conductThe SEC defined improper professional conduct by accountants and can censure, suspend, or bar those who violate it (the AICPA supported the rule). Arthur Andersen, Enron’s auditor, dissolved after severe sanctions. Sarbanes-Oxley strengthened the SEC’s power further, and the SEC has charged EY and several partners with violating auditor independence rules.
Managerial implications
  • Managers study the statements to judge operating efficiency and financial strength.
  • A common technique compares current statements with prior ones to spot trends; large businesses also compare with others in their industry.
  • Managers must understand what the classified groupings mean.
  • Close promptly so new-period transactions can be recorded and information like the cash position stays current.
  • Efficient adjusting and closing, with detailed journal descriptions, makes the annual audit easier.

Rules to remember

Quick review before the practice questions
  • Net sales − COGS = gross profit; gross profit − operating expenses = income from operations.
  • COGS = beginning inventory + net delivered cost − ending inventory.
  • Current = within one year; long-term = more than a year.
  • Close credit-balance accounts, then debit-balance accounts, then Income Summary, then Drawing.
  • Reverse only accrual adjustments tied to future cash flows.

Practice questions

Try each one before you reveal the answer
1.Net sales $200,000; COGS $120,000; operating expenses $50,000. What are gross profit and income from operations?
Show answer

Answer: Gross profit $80,000; income from operations $30,000.

How: Gross profit = $200,000 − $120,000 = $80,000. That’s what’s left after paying for the merchandise. Income from operations = $80,000 − $50,000 = $30,000, after the costs of running the business.

Next step: Add other income and subtract other expenses (like interest) to get net income.

Key takeaway: Net sales → gross profit → income from operations → net income.
2.Current assets $90,000; current liabilities $36,000. Find working capital and the current ratio.
Show answer

Answer: Working capital $54,000; current ratio 2.5 to 1.

How: Working capital = $90,000 − $36,000 = $54,000. Current ratio = $90,000 ÷ $36,000 = 2.5.

What they mean: Both measure whether the business can pay bills coming due soon. Working capital gives the dollar cushion; the ratio says there’s $2.50 of current assets for each $1 of current debt, and is easier to compare across businesses of different sizes.

Key takeaway: Subtract for working capital; divide for the current ratio.
3.Explain the difference between a single-step and a multiple-step income statement. Which is generally favored?
Show answer

Answer: Single-step lists all revenue in one section and all costs and expenses in another, then subtracts once. Multiple-step computes several subtotals (net sales, gross profit, income from operations) before net income. Multiple-step is generally favored.

Why: The subtotals answer useful questions. Gross profit shows whether pricing covers merchandise cost. Income from operations shows how the core business is doing, separate from things like interest.

Key takeaway: Multiple-step tells more of the story.
4.Net credit sales $180,000; average accounts receivable $20,000. Find A/R turnover and the average collection period.
Show answer

Answer: Turnover 9 times; collection period about 40.6 days.

How: Turnover = $180,000 ÷ $20,000 = 9. Collection period = 365 ÷ 9 ≈ 40.6 days.

What it means: On average customers pay about 41 days after a sale. If the credit terms are n/30, customers are paying late, and management might tighten credit or follow up faster.

Key takeaway: Turnover counts collection cycles; 365 ÷ turnover gives days.
5.Why wouldn’t a photocopy machine used in the office be a current asset?
Show answer

Answer: It will be used in the business for more than a year, so it belongs in plant and equipment.

Why: Current assets are cash or items that will become cash or be used up within a year: receivables, inventory, supplies. The copier isn’t for sale and won’t be used up quickly; it helps the business operate for years and is depreciated over that time.

Key takeaway: Used for longer than a year → plant and equipment.
6.Which adjustments are commonly reversed, and why?
Show answer

Answer: Accrued expenses and accrued income that involve future cash payments or receipts, such as accrued salaries and accrued interest.

Why: Example: $1,200 of salaries was accrued on December 31, and the full $1,700 payroll is paid January 3. Without a reversal, the January clerk must remember to debit Salaries Payable $1,200 and Salaries Expense only $500. With a January 1 reversal, the clerk records the payroll normally, and the accounts still come out right. Adjustments with no later cash flow (depreciation, uncollectibles, inventory, prepaid expenses) are never reversed.

Key takeaway: Reverse accruals that lead to a later cash payment or receipt.
